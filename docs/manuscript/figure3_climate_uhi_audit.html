Proposed Supplementary Methods: temperature reconstruction and UHI sensitivity

The 2050 (25/5°C) and 2070 (28/5°C) configurations were retained as illustrative warm-climate scenarios. The 5°C maximum UHI is not an estimate of representative July daily-maximum urban–rural contrast. Daily maximum was the primary statistic in the observational pilot to match the July Tmax climate reference; daytime and night-time means provided separate diagnostic checks.

For fixed land cover and other inputs, InVEST calculates unmixed temperature as T_ref + (1 − HM) × UHI_max, where HM is the heat-mitigation index, followed by normalized spatial mixing. We reconstructed each 22/5°C field as T25_5 − 3°C and the baseline 22/2°C field as 22 + (2/5) × (Tbaseline25_5 − 25). The translation property was checked against executed 25/5°C and 28/5°C runs for all ten land-use configurations; the maximum cell discrepancy from a 3°C shift was 0.000018°C. Comparisons used 15,733,956 common valid London cells. Panel a subtracts the baseline 22/2°C field; panel b subtracts baseline land use at 25/5°C. Cell temperatures were averaged within each borough, then equally across the 33 boroughs. Geographic variation was not interpreted as propagated model uncertainty.

To assess sensitivity to maximum UHI, we ran temperature-only configurations at T_ref = 25°C and UHI_max = 2 and 3°C for baseline land use and all nine alternative scenarios, and compared these with existing 5°C outputs. Land cover, evapotranspiration, biophysical parameters and spatial settings were held fixed. This diagnostic used area-weighted London means on the common valid grid and borough-specific contrasts, distinct from Fig. 3’s equal-weight borough summary. Scenario rankings were compared across settings. No energy, health or work-capacity sensitivity was calculated; temperature-ranking stability therefore does not establish robustness of downstream magnitudes.

Proposed Results

Greening reduced modelled temperatures but did not offset the increase relative to the 2021 reference configuration under the assumed 2050 (25/5°C) and 2070 (28/5°C) climate conditions (Fig. 3a). Under the assumed 2050 conditions, AllBuilt and TreeRisk increased mean temperature across boroughs by 1.05°C and 0.10°C, respectively, whereas TreeOpp reduced it by 0.06°C. Cooling increased with canopy expansion and was greater under Target than Green scenarios: reductions were 0.12, 0.23 and 0.34°C for Green10, Green20 and Green30, compared with 0.14, 0.26 and 0.38°C for Target10, Target20 and Target30 (Fig. 3b). All six greening scenarios cooled every borough, with spatial variation in the magnitude of cooling. At a fixed reference temperature of 25°C, varying maximum UHI from 2 to 5°C preserved the ranking of all nine land-use scenarios, although absolute cooling scaled with UHI magnitude.

Author clarifications and holds 11 November

Holds: Abstract revision waits until the main sections are addressed. Further manuscript-review work is paused until explicitly requested. The clarifications here do not edit Word, rerun models or change figures.

Borough variation is not model uncertainty

The historical temperature notebook calculates the mean across 33 boroughs, then mean ± 1.96 × SD/√33. Its SD describes differences between borough outputs, not uncertainty in temperature inputs, cooling parameters, model structure or canopy assumptions. Under independent random sampling, this is an approximate sampling interval for a population mean. Here all 33 London boroughs are included, and adjacent boroughs are spatially related. It cannot be described as propagated “95% confidence intervals for model estimates.” The arithmetic is not inherently wrong; the interpretation is the problem.

For example, perfectly known modelled cooling of 0.1°C in one borough and 0.5°C in another has spatial variation without uncertainty about those two model outputs. Conversely, all borough outputs can be similar while sharing a large uncertainty in the same UHI parameter: the old error bars would be small even though predictions are uncertain. The original panel b also averages the period-specific means and interval bounds; that is not a new propagated uncertainty interval.

Recommended reporting: deterministic London mean without uncertainty bars; show borough points/boxplots or ranges separately. If retaining the original bar figure, remove its uncertainty bars in a future reporting version, or explicitly label the retained legacy bars as borough-based normal-approximation intervals under an independence assumption, not model uncertainty. For genuine model uncertainty, define plausible input/parameter distributions, propagate joint draws through the relevant model chain, and summarize the resulting London means. A few sensitivity settings or GCMs alone are not automatically a probabilistic 95% interval. No such new analysis is implemented here.

Why CHELSA 2021 minus future WorldClim needs qualification

The subtraction is a valid difference between the selected datasets. It is not automatically a clean estimate of long-term climate change: a single July can be warmer or cooler than its contemporary climatology, and different products can have different spatial calibration/downscaling biases. The result mixes those offsets with the change in climate. WorldClim states that its future data are 20-year means calibrated to WorldClim v2.1, whose historical climate baseline is 1970–2000. This calibration does not automatically harmonize a CHELSA single-year baseline.

Illustration, not London results: future climatology 26°C minus a 2021 value of 22°C gives 4°C. If the matching long-term baseline is 23°C, the anomaly relative to that baseline is 3°C. The additional 1°C need not be future warming.

Author decision, superseding the initial proposal: retain CHELSA July 2021 for the main Figure 1 differences and Figure 2. Add historical WorldClim as a separate reference and use matching-WorldClim differences in the supplement. The download, checks, numerical comparison and proposed labels are in the reference comparison. Do not interpret either historical reference as an interchangeable measurement.

Prescribed conditions do not have to come from Figure 1

Correction to earlier wording: directly deriving Figure 3a from Figure 1 is not a requirement. A sensitivity experiment can legitimately prescribe rural reference temperature and maximum UHI independently. What matters is not describing that experiment as a calibrated projection of the same SSP/year conditions without establishing the link.

For example, raising reference temperature from 25°C to 28°C asks how outcomes respond under a 3°C warmer background; it does not establish that this is London in 2070. Likewise, changing UHI from 2°C to 5°C imposes an additional urban–rural contrast. It is not a future change inferred from Figure 1. Figure 1’s London borough temperature is also not interchangeable with UCM’s rural reference temperature.

Two defensible options remain: (1) retain the old panel-a style as a prescribed climate/UHI sensitivity and label the combinations as such, avoiding unsupported calendar forecasts; or (2) use the new matched-condition two-panel Figure 3 for land-use effects and keep Figure 1 as climate context. A directly coupled forecast is a third, more demanding choice requiring rural-reference and UHI justification. No further change to Figure 3a is made without agreement.

Equal borough weights versus London area weights

Neither mean is inherently wrong. Equal borough weights answer “what is the average borough’s modelled change?”; area weights answer “what is the average change over London’s land area?”. For a 10 km² borough cooling by 1°C and a 90 km² borough cooling by 0.1°C, the equal-borough mean is 0.55°C, while the area-weighted mean is 0.19°C. Neither is a resident-weighted exposure estimate.

For revised Green30/Target30, equal-borough cooling is 0.3364/0.3780°C and area-weighted cooling is 0.2910/0.3192°C. This difference changes magnitudes, not their revised ranking. Recommend area weighting for claims about London’s land-area mean, borough means/distributions for geographic comparison, and explicit labels for both. The error-bar interpretation is a separate issue: changing weights does not by itself propagate model uncertainty.

3. UHI source and interpretation

InVEST computes unmixed temperature as T_ref + (1 − HM) × UHI_max, then spatially mixes the result. Its reference is rural air temperature, and its UHI parameter is an urban–rural air-temperature contrast. The public InVEST guide also lists the Yale explorer as a possible source; that suggestion does not make surface and air-temperature measures equivalent. The installed 3.20.2 equation was checked directly; the accessible web guide is version 3.20.0.

The cited Yale explorer uses satellite land-surface temperature and reports cluster-averaged UHI statistics. This audit does not establish which London extraction, season, year or statistic yielded 2 and 5°C, or validate them against air measurements. The manuscript’s “average and maximum” values do not establish a temporal increase from present to future. Treat the 5°C setting as a prescribed scenario assumption until its air-temperature relevance is demonstrated.

This matters beyond labels: with heat-mitigation fields and mixing held fixed, the land-use temperature contrast scales with UHI_max; changing reference temperature alone cancels from the contrast. A 2°C versus 5°C UHI contrast would therefore scale the temperature benefit by about 0.4, under those fixed conditions. This is an algebraic sensitivity illustration, not a new calibrated result. Health responses and work capacity need their own calculations if parameters change; do not scale every downstream outcome by the same factor.
